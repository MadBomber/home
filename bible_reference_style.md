# Bible Reference Style

How to write chapter-and-verse references, summarized from Felix Just, S.J.,
[*Biblical References: Format, Examples, History*](https://www.catholic-resources.org/Bible/Biblical_References.htm)
(Catholic Resources, last updated 2021-11-12). The rules and examples below
follow that page. The last section compares them with what this site's studies
do today.

---

## When and Where to Cite

- **Back up every claim** with a specific reference: the book name followed by
  the chapter and verse numbers.
- **Cite passages you mention, not only passages you quote.**
- **Put the reference at the end of the sentence, in parentheses**, before the
  closing punctuation.
- **Put it inside the sentence, without parentheses**, when the reference is
  part of what you are claiming.
- **Signal words:**

  | Word | Meaning | Use it when |
  |---|---|---|
  | `see` | see | pointing the reader to a supporting text |
  | `cf.` | compare | pointing to a similar *or* contrasting text |
  | `e.g.,` | for example | the list is a sample, not complete |
  | `par.` | parallel | pointing to the same account in another book (especially the Gospels) |

- **Book names:** spell out the full name in running text. In parenthetical
  references, use the standard short American abbreviation.
- **Dropping the book name:** after the first reference, you may leave out the
  book name while you are clearly still in the same book. Once more than one
  book is in play, name the book every time.
- **No italics and no periods** in American book abbreviations.

## Examples in Sentences

These are the source's own examples.

> Have you read the parables of Jesus, such as the famous parable of the Good
> Samaritan (Luke 10:30-37)?

A reference in parentheses at the end of the sentence.

> The Gospel according to Mark is the first Christian text that uses the word
> "gospel" or "good news" (1:1).

The book is named in the sentence, so the reference gives only chapter and
verse.

> The death of Jesus is briefly but powerfully described in the Fourth Gospel:
> "When Jesus had received the wine, he said, 'It is finished.' Then he bowed his
> head and gave up his spirit" (John 19:30).

A direct quotation, with the reference after the closing quotation mark and
before the period.

> Matthew usually uses the phrase "Kingdom of Heaven" rather than "Kingdom of
> God" (e.g., Matt 3:2; 4:17; 5:3; etc.; but cf. 6:33; 12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43).

`e.g.` for a sample list and `cf.` for contrasting texts. The book is named
once and then dropped.

> The feeding of the 5000 is found in all four canonical Gospels (Mark 6:32-44;
> par. Matt 14:13-21; Luke 9:10b-17; John 6:1-15).

`par.` introduces the parallel accounts. `9:10b` means the second part of
verse 10.

> The Fourth Gospel begins with the same two words as the LXX Greek version of
> Genesis (cf. John 1:1 and Gen 1:1).

`cf.` asks the reader to compare two texts in different books, so both books
are named.

## Punctuation Rules (American System)

| Mark | Goes between | Example |
|---|---|---|
| colon `:` | chapter and verse | `John 9:12` |
| comma `,` | verses in the same chapter, or chapters when no verses are given | `John 9:1, 12`; `John 9, 12` |
| semicolon `;` | references in different chapters (with verses) | `John 9:1; 12:36` |
| hyphen `-` | a verse range inside one chapter | `John 9:1-12` |
| en dash `–` | a range that crosses chapters, or a range of whole chapters | `John 9:1–12:36`; `John 9–12` |
| space | the book abbreviation and the chapter number (no period) | `Matt 3:2`, not `Matt.3:2` |

The source's text allows a single hyphen for a range of whole chapters (`9-12`),
but its own table prints `John 9–12` with the longer dash, as shown above.
Spaces around the longer dash are optional; the source says it "does not matter
much."

## Reference Formats

The American form is the one to use. The European form is shown for
comparison, because it swaps the roles of the comma and the period.

| American | Meaning | European |
|---|---|---|
| `John 9` | chapter 9 | `John 9` |
| `John 9, 12` | chapters 9 and 12 only | `John 9; 12` |
| `John 9–12` | chapters 9 through 12 (four chapters) | `John 9–12` |
| `John 9:12` | chapter 9, verse 12 | `John 9,12` |
| `John 9:12b` | chapter 9, the second part of verse 12 | `John 9,12b` |
| `John 9:1, 12` | chapter 9, verses 1 and 12 only | `John 9,1.12` |
| `John 9:1-12` | chapter 9, verses 1 through 12 | `John 9,1-12` |
| `John 9:1-12, 36` | chapter 9, verses 1 through 12, plus verse 36 | `John 9,1-12.36` |
| `John 9:1; 12:36` | two single verses: 9:1 and 12:36 | `John 9,1; 12,36` |
| `John 9:1–12:36` | the whole section from 9:1 to 12:36 | `John 9,1-12,36` |
| `John 9:1-12; 12:3-6` | two passages: 9:1-12 and 12:3-6 | `John 9,1-12; 12,3-6` |
| `John 9:1-3, 6-12; 12:3-6` | three passages: 9:1-3, 9:6-12, and 12:3-6 | `John 9,1-3.6-12; 12,3-6` |
| `John 9:12-13` | verses 12 and 13 ("12 and following") | `John 9,12f` |
| *(not used)* | verse 12 "and the following verses", open-ended | `John 9,12ff` |

**Avoid `f` and `ff`.** Give the exact verses instead. `ff` leaves the end of the
passage unstated.

## Easily Confused Names

Watch books whose names or abbreviations look alike:

| Written | Means |
|---|---|
| `John 1` | the Gospel of John, chapter 1 |
| `1 John` | the First Letter of John (all of it) |
| `1 John 2` | the First Letter of John, chapter 2 |
| `1 & 2 John` | the First and Second Letters of John |
| `John 1 & 2` | the Gospel of John, chapters 1 and 2 (usually written `John 1-2`) |

| Short forms that collide | Books |
|---|---|
| `Ex` / `Ez` / `Ezr` | Exodus / Ezekiel / Ezra |
| `Hb` / `Heb` | Habakkuk / Hebrews |
| `Jon` / `Jn` | Jonah / John |
| `Phil` / `Phlm` | Philippians / Philemon |
| `Ti` / `Tim` | Titus / Timothy |

To avoid collisions, prefer the slightly longer abbreviations or the full short
names: **Exod, Ezek, Ezra, Hab, Jonah, John, Titus**.

## Book Titles and One-Chapter Books

- A few books go by more than one title: *Song of Songs* is also *Canticle of
  Canticles* or *Song of Solomon*, and *Revelation* is also *the Revelation to
  John* or *the Apocalypse*.
- Five books have only one chapter: **Obadiah, Philemon, 2 John, 3 John, and
  Jude**.
- Modern chapter numbers go back to Stephen Langton (d. 1228). New Testament
  verse numbers come from Robert Estienne (1551). The Geneva Bible (1560) was
  the first English Bible to carry both.

## How This Site Compares

This is where the site's studies (ntc1y, ot1y) already differ from the source.
It records the differences; it does not change the study style guides.

| Point | Source (American system) | This site today |
|---|---|---|
| Book names in parentheses | abbreviations: `(Matt 14:13-21)` | full names: `(Matthew 14:13-21)` |
| Cross-chapter range | en dash: `Genesis 32:1–36:43` | plain hyphen: `Genesis 32:1-36:43` (ot1y_STYLE.md: "never an en dash inside a reference") |
| Whole-chapter range | en dash: `Leviticus 1–3` | written with verses: `Leviticus 1:1-3:17` |
| Parallel accounts | inline `par.` | a separate `parallel_passages` field and paragraph |
| One-chapter books | the source gives no rule | ot1y writes `Jude 14-15`; BibleGateway accepts both `Jude 14-15` and `Jude 1:14-15` |
| Same-book shorthand | drop the book after the first mention | `Genesis 21:22-34; Genesis 22:1-8` in front matter (each list item stands alone); bare `(22:8)` in prose |
