A jury returns a guilty verdict, and the defendant does not go straight to a cell. There is a gap, sometimes weeks long, between the verdict and the sentencing hearing where the actual penalty gets read out loud. He goes home in the interim, in most cases. He eats dinner. He talks to his lawyer, argues about the details of a future that is no longer genuinely in question, prepares for a day whose outcome has already been decided even though its exact terms have not yet been spoken aloud. Nothing about the outcome is in doubt during that gap. The finding has already been made, on the record, by the people whose job it was to make it. And yet the gap is real, has to be lived through by everyone in the room, including the man whose fate is no longer in question. He walks out of the courthouse that day. He is still guilty. Those two facts are not in tension with each other.

That is closer to the truth about Satan than any battlefield image gets. A battlefield image assumes an outcome still being decided in real time, momentum shifting, either side capable of the final blow right up until the last exchange. That is not the shape of this story. This is not a fight with an undecided outcome, moving toward some climax still genuinely up for grabs. The verdict was entered a long time ago. What is left is the gap, and how to live inside it.

## The Verdict

The sentence gets handed down almost immediately after the first sin, before Adam and Eve have even left the garden, spoken directly to the serpent:

> "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel."
>
> Genesis 3:15 (ESV)

Read past the parallel structure and the verse looks symmetrical, a bruise traded for a bruise, even odds. Look closer and the symmetry breaks. A wound to the head and a wound to the heel are not the same kind of wound. Step on a snake's head, and the snake is finished. Get struck on the heel, and you are hurt, badly, but you are still standing, still walking, still able to finish whatever you were doing when the strike landed. One wound ends something. The other one is survived. The sentence names both blows inside a single breath, and the asymmetry inside that single breath is the whole outcome, stated before either wound has landed, before humanity's story has done more than take its first bad step. Whatever this cost the offspring of the woman, and the rest of the story makes clear exactly what it cost, the wound was never going to be the kind that ends anything for Him.

## The Trial Already Happened

Genesis 3:15 is a sentence handed down in advance. The cross is the moment it stops being a forecast and becomes a fact entered on the record.

> "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."
>
> Colossians 2:15 (ESV)

Name plainly the scene where that happens: not a private spiritual transaction happening somewhere unseen, but a cross, a public execution, the least private death Rome knew how to stage. Colossians describes exactly that moment as the site of the actual defeat. Disarmed. Put to open shame. That is not private, comforting language, the kind of thing said quietly to reassure someone in doubt. It is legal and military language, the vocabulary of a public verdict rendered where everyone can see it, not a private assurance whispered to the anxious. Every limit already visible in how Satan operates, the leash he works inside, the power he can only borrow, the ground he can only use if someone hands it to him, gets formally entered here as a judgment rather than simply observed as a recurring pattern. Those limits were always true of him, the way a counterfeit is always a counterfeit whether or not anyone has checked it under the right light yet. The cross is the moment the checking happened publicly, on the record, in front of everyone, rather than remaining a fact only God could see. The trial is not still in progress. It already happened, and it did not go his way.

## The Sentence Still to Come

What has not happened yet is the sentence itself, and Scripture is specific about where it lands.

> "and the devil who had deceived them was thrown into the lake of fire and sulfur where the beast and the false prophet were, and they will be tormented day and night forever and ever."
>
> Revelation 20:10 (ESV)

Paul writes to a first-century church with the same outcome, phrased as something close at hand:

> "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet."
>
> Romans 16:20 (ESV)

Soon. That word deserves to be addressed directly rather than slid past, because two thousand years is a long time for "soon" to still be pending, and a gap that wide deserves better than a hand wave. Here is the honest answer: a delay is not the same thing as a lapsed verdict. A court calendar that runs slower than expected does not un-convict anyone; the guilty finding does not get weaker with each passing month on the docket. What "soon" measures is not a countdown running against a wall clock the way an oven timer does. It is closer to a fixed date on a calendar nobody but the judge can see, announced to a church that was promised the outcome, not the date. The wait can be long and the verdict can still be exactly as settled as the day it was rendered. Those are not competing claims. They are the same claim, described from two different points inside the same gap.

There is a version of this that gets treated as an embarrassment, the kind of unanswered promise skeptics like to point at, but the embarrassment only lands if "soon" was ever meant to function as a stopwatch. Peter addresses the exact objection directly, by name, rather than dodging it:

> "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance."
>
> 2 Peter 3:9 (ESV)

A page earlier he had already reset the whole scale of the measurement: "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8). Two thousand years of human calendar time is not evidence against the verdict. It is simply what "soon" looks like measured from inside the wait instead of from wherever the promise was made, and Scripture names that gap in scale before a single skeptic ever gets the chance to point at it.

## He Knows It Too

Whatever confidence gets borrowed from that verdict, Satan does not get to borrow the opposite. He is not in suspense about how this goes, and Revelation states it plainly, in the same scene where he is cast down:

> "And the great dragon was thrown down, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world—he was thrown down to the earth, and his angels were thrown down with him."
>
> Revelation 12:9 (ESV)

> "Therefore, rejoice, O heavens and you who dwell in them! But woe to you, O earth and sea, for the devil has come down to you in great wrath, because he knows that his time is short!"
>
> Revelation 12:12 (ESV)

"Great wrath" is not the wrath of a being who might still pull out a win. It is the wrath of a being who already knows the finding and is working against a window he can feel closing. That reframes the danger of the present moment without minimizing it: a cornered opponent who knows exactly how little time is left is not a safer opponent to be careless around. He is a more urgent one, precisely because he has nothing left to lose by spending whatever time remains as destructively as he can.

Worth being precise about the source of that knowledge, since it is easy to assume the wrong kind. He did not calculate the ending through his own foresight, the way a strategist reads a battlefield and predicts how it will end. He was told. Genesis 3:15 announced it to him directly, centuries before Revelation ever describes him reacting to it. What Revelation 12:12 records is not discovery. It is a being watching a deadline he has known about the entire time finally arrive within sight.

## Until an Opportune Time

There is a word for the shape of that remaining time, and it shows up at the exact turning point where the wilderness temptation ends:

> "And when the devil had ended every temptation, he departed from him until an opportune time."
>
> Luke 4:13 (ESV)

The Greek behind "opportune time" is *kairos*, a fitting moment, an occasion ripe for something, not simply *chronos*, the ordinary passage of duration one hour after another. Two different words exist for two different kinds of time, and Luke reaches for the one that names an occasion rather than a duration. He is not saying the devil left and time passed. He is saying the devil left with an eye already on the next window, flagged inside the temptation narrative itself as unfinished business rather than a closed case.

That window keeps recurring, and it recurs in places nobody would expect to find it. Peter earns the same rebuke Satan himself would, "Get behind me, Satan!" (Matthew 16:23), spoken to the disciple who had just confessed Jesus as the Christ a few verses earlier (Matthew 16:16). One *kairos* landing inside the mouth of a friend, mid-conversation, rather than announcing itself as an obvious attack from an obvious stranger. Judas supplies another; whatever "entered into" (Luke 22:3; John 13:27) did and did not override in him, the data point stands on its own here: a window opened, and something walked through it. What matters is the shape those recurrences trace together. The gap between Luke 4:13 and Revelation 20:10 is not empty waiting. It is one long, continuous stretch of *kairos* moments, one after another, and the whole of church history, including whatever week is currently being lived through, sits inside that same unbroken stretch.

## Living Between the Verdict and the Sentence

That is the actual question worth asking, day to day. Not "should I fear him," an already-answered question next to a leashed, borrowed, countdown-bound opponent. The sharper question is how to live inside a gap where the outcome is settled but the clock has not yet run out.

Two matched errors sit on either side of the honest answer, and neither deserves to pass as reasonable. The first treats the length of the gap as evidence the verdict might still be overturned, functional doubt dressed up as patience, as though enough delay could eventually undo a finding that was never in question. It shows up as a background hum of anxiety about whether the story really does end the way it says it does, a hum that gets louder every time the gap stretches longer than expected. The second error runs the opposite direction: it treats the certainty of the outcome as a reason not to bother resisting anything in the meantime, functional passivity dressed up as confidence, a misuse of a settled ending as an excuse to stop showing up for the parts of the story still being written. If the ending is already fixed, this error reasons, then today's small compromise or today's skipped resistance costs nothing that was not already accounted for.

Neither error is harmless, and they are worth naming as two different failures rather than one, because they get corrected by opposite means. The first lives in low-grade anxiety about a case that is not open, and it gets corrected by the verdict itself, read plainly, repeatedly, until the anxiety has nothing left to feed on. The second lives in a complacency James already ruled out: "resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7) was never suspended just because the war's outcome stopped being in doubt. A settled ending was never permission to stop fighting the parts still in front of you. It was the reason the fighting was never futile in the first place.

Paul's own audience makes the point without needing to be told twice. He does not tell the Roman church to sit back and watch the crushing happen from a safe distance, someday, to somebody else. He tells them the crushing happens "under your feet." Their feet. A church made up of ordinary people, most of them with no idea they were living inside a story that would still be running two thousand years later, gets handed a promise phrased as their own participation, not a spectacle to be observed from the stands. Present participants in a fight whose final score was never up for grabs, standing in exactly the kind of gap being described here, expected to keep standing in it rather than to treat the certainty of the ending as permission to stop. The verdict does not do the standing for anyone. It only guarantees what the standing is for.

A convicted man walking out of a courthouse before sentencing does not get to act as though the verdict never happened. He also is not living in a meaningless interval just because the actual penalty has not been read aloud yet. The walk between those two moments is not neutral time killed while waiting for something else to become real. It is part of how he is found, when the sentence finally does get read. What that walk looks like, who it is spent with, what gets built or wasted during it, all of that belongs to the gap and to nothing else. It is not a rehearsal for some more real chapter still to come. It is where the actual living happens, verdict already entered, sentence still pending, both facts true in the same breath, the same asymmetry Genesis 3:15 named on the very first page of this story.

Every scene where Satan appears in Scripture shows him working inside a boundary someone else drew, a leash, a wall he cannot climb, ground he can only work if it is handed to him, power he can only borrow and never own outright, a creature who has to walk to be present anywhere at all and cannot know a story's ending except by being told it in advance. This scene names that same boundary as a timeline instead of a wall. Not just where he cannot go. When his story runs out. The verdict was entered a long time ago. The gap is real, and it is still being lived in, right now. How it gets lived in is the only part of the story that is still being written.
