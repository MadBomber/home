Some accounts in the Bible are just weird.

Jesus curses a fig tree for having no fruit, and it dies. God strikes a man dead for reaching out to steady the ark of the covenant. A prophet is jeered by a crowd of boys, curses them, and two bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them. Read cold, with nothing around them, these land somewhere between baffling and disturbing. The reflex is to file them all under one heading, the Bible is strange, and move along. Or worse, to feel a quiet embarrassment about them, the way you might about a relative who says things you would rather not have to explain to your friends.

There is a better response than either one, and it begins by taking the strangeness seriously instead of managing it.

The strangeness is real. What it is not, almost ever, is a defect in the text. It is a signal. It marks the exact spot where you are standing outside the frame that would make the account make sense: a piece of context you never had, a symbol you did not recognize, or something about God Himself that your first instinct measured wrong.

That last one is usually where the trouble starts.

> "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, declares the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways and my thoughts than your thoughts."
>
> Isaiah 55:8-9 (ESV)

When an account strikes you as arbitrary or unfair, that verdict is being handed down by a particular judge. You. Your sense of what is reasonable, what is proportionate, what a fair God would and would not do. Isaiah says the plain thing: that sense is not the measure. The distance between your reasoning and His is the distance between the ground and the sky.

It would be easy to read that as a door closing. Stop asking. You will never understand, so do not try. It says the opposite. If His thoughts run higher than yours, then the answer to what looks arbitrary is not missing. It is up there, higher than your first reaction reached. Which means the reaction was never the last word on the matter. It was the first one, offered too early.

> "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame."
>
> Proverbs 18:13 (ESV)

That is what "this is weird" almost always is. An answer given before hearing. A verdict on an account you have not read to the end, in a setting you have not supplied, about a God whose reasons you assumed you already knew. Proverbs does not call that clever, or skeptical, or intellectually honest. It calls it folly and shame, the posture of a man answering a question while the other party is still talking.

So this collection does the opposite. It hears each account out.

Every essay takes one of these strange accounts, the kind that makes a reader wince or a skeptic grin, and refuses to answer before it has heard the whole thing. It reads the verse inside its chapter. It supplies the history the first readers already had. It asks what the God behind the account was doing, instead of what a cornered defender needs Him to have done. And every time, the account stops being arbitrary. Not because the strangeness gets explained away, but because it finally gets explained. The thing you were ready to be embarrassed by turns out to be doing something you would have wanted, if you had seen the whole of it.

Weird is mostly the name we give to context we do not have yet.

That is the walk these essays take, one account at a time. Hear the whole thing. Then decide whether it was ever strange, or only unfinished in your memory.

**Weird Bible Stories: Collection Contents**

[Part 1: Are You a Fig Tree?](/blog/are-you-a-fig-tree/)
Jesus curses a fig tree for having no fruit, and read alone it looks petty and unfair. Read whole, on the page Mark actually wrote it on, it was never about a tree. It is about the difference between leaves and figs, and what happens to anything that shows the one without the other.

[Part 2: Just Trying to Help](/blog/just-trying-to-help/)
God strikes a man dead for reaching out to steady the ark, and read alone the account looks arbitrary and cruel. Read whole, across the centuries it spans, the offense turns out to be in the reading, not in God. The hand that reached to hold God up had it exactly backwards.

[Part 3: Elisha and the Two Bears](/blog/elisha-and-the-two-bears/)
A crowd dares a prophet to vanish the way his master had that morning, and two bears maul them for it. Read alone it looks like God backing a man's wounded pride. Read whole, the bears turn out to be the Old Covenant's own terms, agreed to at Sinai centuries earlier, and the line dividing bears from bread is not age but how much a person had been told. The same dare returns once more, at a cross, where the answer is mercy instead of fire.
