Some men at a Bible study have a joke they tell their wives. Fishing for money is in the Bible. So a new boat for next season's tournaments is practically a scriptural expense.

It is a good joke, and it works because the account is real. Jesus tells a fisherman to cast a hook, take the first fish that comes up, and open its mouth. There is a coin inside.

Look at what the fish paid. One coin. Not a purse, not a catch worth selling, not a second coin for the next trip. The coin is spent the moment it is found, on a tax bill, on two names. Nobody in the account gets a boat. Peter does not go back to the lake hunting for another fish, and Matthew never says he did. The fish gave exact change.

That is the strange part, and it is not the fish. The man who fed five thousand from five loaves and two fish (Matthew 14:13-21) could have settled this bill any way He liked. He could have produced the coin from a pocket, or from nowhere, or told the collectors to wait. He sends a fisherman to the water for one shekel.

Only one of the four Gospels records it, and it is the only book in the New Testament that uses the word *stater*, the Greek name for the coin. That Gospel was written by a man who used to collect taxes for a living (Matthew 9:9). He knew what a tax bill was, and who had to pay it.

The collectors had asked a simple question. The answer to it costs a fish.

## Yes

The question comes in Capernaum, from men who collect for the Temple.

> "When they came to Capernaum, the collectors of the two-drachma tax went up to Peter and said, 'Does your teacher not pay the tax?' He said, 'Yes.'"
>
> Matthew 17:24-25 (ESV)

The two-drachma tax is a half-shekel, a small payment for the upkeep of the sanctuary, and it traces back to Moses (Exodus 30:11-16). The collectors do not ask Jesus. They ask Peter, and they ask in a way that already expects the answer. Does your teacher not pay?

Peter gives them one word. Yes.

He is not wrong about the outcome. Jesus pays. What he did is answer for another man before that man had said anything on the matter. Scripture has a line for exactly this.

> "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame."
>
> Proverbs 18:13 (ESV)

The verse stops short of calling Peter a fool. What it names is a speed: the answer arrived before the hearing did.

And the hearing arrives. When Peter goes into the house, Matthew writes that "Jesus spoke to him first." The Greek verb, *prophthanō*, means to get ahead of someone, to anticipate. It appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Before Peter can report what the collectors asked, the question is already in Jesus' hands. The text does not say how He knew. It says He was there before Peter was.

## What Do You Think, Simon?

Jesus leaves the collectors' question unanswered and asks Peter one of His own.

> "Jesus spoke to him first, saying, 'What do you think, Simon? From whom do kings of the earth take toll or tax? From their sons or from others?' And when he said, 'From others,' Jesus said to him, 'Then the sons are free.'"
>
> Matthew 17:25-26 (ESV)

Peter supplies the answer. Jesus asks, and Peter says it himself: from others. Kings tax their subjects, not their children. A king's son does not stand in line at the king's tax table, because the house the money supports is his own.

So the question was never about Capernaum. It was about whose house the Temple is. Peter has already heard the answer to that, and recently. He was one of three men Jesus took up a high mountain (Matthew 17:1), and he was standing there when the cloud came down.

> "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him."
>
> Matthew 17:5 (ESV)

The Father said it in Peter's hearing. The last two words of it were an instruction. Listen to him. Peter comes down the mountain, meets two tax collectors, and says yes before he has listened to anything.

The exchange in the house turns. The Temple tax is paid to the Father's house, and the Son is asked to pay it. The only man in Capernaum with no obligation on that ledger is the one the collectors are checking on. Free, by His own argument. Asked to pay, by theirs.

He will pay. The coin still has to come from somewhere, and the reason it exists at all is older than Capernaum, older than the Temple, and not about money.

## The Price of Being Counted

The tax has a history, and it starts at Sinai with a headcount. God tells Moses what is to happen whenever Israel is numbered.

> "When you take the census of the people of Israel, then each shall give a ransom for his life to the LORD when you number them, that there be no plague among them when you number them."
>
> Exodus 30:12 (ESV)

Read that slowly. Something is at risk when God's people are numbered, and the text does not say what. It names the danger, a plague, and it names the remedy, a payment. The payment is "a ransom for his life." The Hebrew word is *kopher*, a price that covers a life. It comes from a verb meaning to cover, and a ransom is exactly that: the amount laid down so that what is owed is covered.

The amount is small. Half a shekel "according to the shekel of the sanctuary" (Exodus 30:13), from everyone counted "from twenty years old and upward" (Exodus 30:14). Then comes the rule that levels every person in the line.

> "The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less, than the half shekel, when you give the LORD's offering to make atonement for your lives."
>
> Exodus 30:15 (ESV)

A life costs the same coin. Wealth cannot buy a larger ransom, and poverty does not excuse a smaller one. A fisherman and his teacher stand at the same price.

The money does not vanish into a treasury. It goes "for the service of the tent of meeting, that it may bring the people of Israel to remembrance before the LORD, so as to make atonement for your lives" (Exodus 30:16). Atonement closes verse 15 and closes verse 16. The coin is a record, not a fee for services rendered: a price was paid for this life, and it is remembered before the LORD.

That is what Matthew means by the two-drachma tax. By his day the census payment had become a yearly levy for the Temple, but the amount was the same half-shekel, counted in Greek coin as two drachmas.[^yearly-levy] The collectors in Capernaum thought they were asking for a tax. They were asking for a ransom, and the man they were asking about was about to say, indoors, that the sons are free.

He will pay it anyway. The only question left is where the coin comes from, and the answer is in the water.

## His Sea

Jesus answers the collectors' question with instructions for a fisherman.

> "However, not to give offense to them, go to the sea and cast a hook and take the first fish that comes up, and when you open its mouth you will find a shekel."
>
> Matthew 17:27 (ESV)

The instruction leaves a great deal out. No searching, no luck, no second cast. The first fish that comes up. Jesus tells Peter where the money is, and the telling takes the place of any search.

Matthew writes the command and stops. No hook goes into the water on the page, no fish is landed, no coin is held up for the collectors. Peter's whole assignment is a hook and a mouth to open.

The sea is Jesus' to send Peter to, and Scripture says why. Psalm 8 lists what was placed under the feet of the one it calls the son of man, and the sea is on the list.

> "You have given him dominion over the works of your hands; you have put all things under his feet, all sheep and oxen, and also the beasts of the field, the birds of the heavens, and the fish of the sea, whatever passes along the paths of the seas."
>
> Psalm 8:6-8 (ESV)

The letter to the Hebrews quotes that psalm and then says where to look for the one it describes.

> "Now in putting everything in subjection to him, he left nothing outside his control. At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him. But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone."
>
> Hebrews 2:8-9 (ESV)

"At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him." The verse admits the sight is partial. Capernaum is a small view of it: one fish, one mouth, one coin, all answering the word of the man Hebrews calls "made lower than the angels" for a little while, and the sea still does as it is told.

The coin comes out of His own water, in the exact size of the debt, and it was never borrowed, begged, or earned.[^need-not-want] Nobody in Capernaum was watching the sea for a payment. That is where it came from.

The last line of the instruction says who the coin is for.

## Me and Yourself

> "Take that and give it to them for me and for yourself."
>
> Matthew 17:27 (ESV)

Count the coins. The tax is a two-drachma tax, owed by each person. The shekel in the fish's mouth is, in the ESV's own note, "a silver coin worth four drachmas." Two drachmas for Jesus. Two drachmas for Peter. One coin covers both names with nothing left over.

The order matters too. "For me and for yourself." He does not say "for us," and He does not put Peter first. The one man in Capernaum who owes nothing names Himself first on the payment, and Peter's name goes in beside His.

Go back to Exodus. Each person counted gave "a ransom for his life." Peter's ransom is in this coin. He did not earn it, find it, or carry it to the water. The coin does not come from his pocket. The only thing he brought to the payment was a hook, and the money that covers his life came out of Another's sea.

Jesus says why He pays at all. It is "not to give offense to them." He is free by His own argument, and He pays anyway, and the reason He gives is not that He owes it. A right He holds is set down so that the collectors do not stumble.

One payer, two names, one coin. Peter's name goes on the record beside His, and Peter did not pay for it.

## Not With Silver or Gold

Matthew set this scene directly after a grim one.

> "As they were gathering in Galilee, Jesus said to them, 'The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men, and they will kill him, and he will be raised on the third day.' And they were greatly distressed."
>
> Matthew 17:22-23 (ESV)

In Capernaum, the man who is free of the ransom pays it. In Galilee, a few verses earlier, the same man says where He is headed. A census numbers people and collects a ransom for each. The one who owes no ransom is about to be numbered with the ones who do. At His last Passover meal, He quoted Isaiah about Himself.

> "For I tell you that this Scripture must be fulfilled in me: 'And he was numbered with the transgressors.'"
>
> Luke 22:37 (ESV)

Three chapters after the fish, in the same Gospel that records the coin, Jesus uses the word ransom about Himself.

> "...even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many."
>
> Matthew 20:28 (ESV)

The half-shekel was a ransom for a life, one coin to a person. This ransom is a life, given for many.

Peter wrote about that same ransom later.

> "...knowing that you were ransomed from the futile ways inherited from your forefathers, not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot."
>
> 1 Peter 1:18-19 (ESV)

The letter opens "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:1). Of all the men who could have written "not with perishable things such as silver or gold," he is the one who was once told where to find a silver coin to pay a ransom. The text does not say he had Capernaum in mind when he wrote it. It does not need to. The coin that covered his name in the house was silver. The price that covered it for good was not.

## Yes, He Pays

Peter owed a temple tax, and Jesus paid it. You and I owe a debt that no coin can touch, one we could never repay if we worked for the rest of our lives. Paul calls it a record of debt, and tells the Colossians what became of it.

> "...by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross."
>
> Colossians 2:14 (ESV)

The coin in Capernaum was a record that a life was covered. This is the record that finally covered it, canceled by the one who paid with His own life. He paid it all.

Listen to the verbs Jesus gave Peter. Take the first fish that comes up. Take that, and give it to them. The coin was already in the fish when Peter went to the water, and his part was a hook, a mouth to open, and a hand. The other payment is set out the same way.

> "For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord."
>
> Romans 6:23 (ESV)

Wages are earned. A gift is taken. What was owed has been paid, and what remains is offered.

The collectors had asked a simple question.

> "Does your teacher not pay the tax?"
>
> Matthew 17:24 (ESV)

Peter said yes, and he said it too soon. He was right anyway. The teacher paid. Nobody in Capernaum got a boat out of it, and the coin in the fish was the smallest part of the bill.

[^need-not-want]: "And my God will supply every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). The verse says need, not want. The man in the joke wants a boat. The fish supplies a shekel, and a shekel was the need. The verse also measures the supply by His riches, not by the size of the need. In Capernaum the need was small and the sea was full.

[^yearly-levy]: Nehemiah 10:32 (the returned exiles pledge a yearly contribution for the service of the house of God, set there at a third of a shekel).
