A counterfeit bill can pass a lot of tests. It can fool a cash register, a clerk in a hurry, a wallet full of real ones sitting next to it. It uses real paper, a real design copied stroke for stroke, real ink laid down in the right pattern, real numbers in the right places. Hold it up to ordinary daylight, run it through most people's hands, and nothing about it announces itself as fake. It was made to survive exactly that kind of casual scrutiny.

What it cannot do is manufacture the one thing the counterfeiter never had access to: the security thread woven into the paper at the mill, the thing a pen test or a UV light is checking for. That detail was never available to steal, because it was never printed on the surface at all. It was built into the material before the counterfeiter ever got near it. The bill can copy everything the original looks like. It cannot copy the one thing that made the original real in the first place, because that part was never the counterfeiter's to have.

Now apply that to the being Scripture calls the accuser, the deceiver, the father of lies. Every place the Bible shows him at work, the same shape shows up. He does not invent. He copies. And every copy has a seam, reached at a specific point, that he did not put there and cannot erase, because he was never the one who set the terms of what he was copying in the first place.

That claim runs directly against the instinct that treats him as a second, dark source of creativity, an inventor operating in his own right, dreaming up temptations and counterfeits from some well of original material God does not have access to. Nothing in Scripture supports that picture. What it shows, every time, in every scene, is a borrower.

## A Wall the Magicians Could Not Cross

Egypt gives the clearest demonstration, because it happens three times before it stops happening at all. Moses turns his staff into a serpent, and Pharaoh summons his own wise men to answer it:

> "Then Pharaoh summoned the wise men and the sorcerers, and they, the magicians of Egypt, also did the same by their secret arts. For each man cast down his staff, and they became serpents. But Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs."
>
> Exodus 7:11-12 (ESV)

Two things happen in that verse, not one, and the second is easy to read past. The magicians really do reproduce the sign; their staffs really become serpents. And then Aaron's staff swallows theirs, a small, almost throwaway detail that states, inside the very scene where the imitation succeeds, which power the imitation is standing inside of. The copy works. It still gets eaten. Moses turns the Nile to blood, and they match that too: "the magicians of Egypt did the same by their secret arts" (Exodus 7:22). Moses brings up frogs, and once more: "the magicians did the same by their secret arts and made frogs come up on the land of Egypt" (Exodus 8:7). Three signs, three successful copies. The text does not treat any of these as stage tricks within its own narrative. Real power reproduced real power, on its own terms, three times running.

Then Moses brings gnats, and the run ends.

> "The magicians tried by their secret arts to produce gnats, but they could not. So there were gnats on man and beast."
>
> Exodus 8:18 (ESV)

They try. The verb matters. Whatever they had been doing for three signs running, they attempt to do again, and this time it does not work. And the confession comes from exactly the people you would expect to minimize the failure:

> "Then the magicians said to Pharaoh, 'This is the finger of God.'"
>
> Exodus 8:19 (ESV)

Not Moses. Not the narrator. The counterfeiters themselves, at the exact moment the copying stops working, naming what they had just run into. Three successes had built an expectation that the imitation was unlimited. It was not, and the shape of the failure is worth a second look. A serpent, a river turned to blood, a plague of frogs, these are dramatic, visible, headline signs, exactly the kind a rival power would be motivated to counterfeit for maximum effect. Gnats are the smallest thing in the entire sequence, barely worth a headline at all. The wall did not show up at the hardest-looking sign. It showed up at the least impressive one, which is a strong argument that the limit was never about difficulty in any sense the magicians could calculate in advance. It was simply where the permission ran out, on a schedule they did not set and could not see coming. Real power, then a wall neither their skill nor their patron's power could see coming or push through.

## The Kingdoms That Were Never His to Build

The same shape returns in the wilderness, in the last of the three temptations, and here the stakes are higher than gnats.

> "and said to him, 'To you I will give all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whom I will.'"
>
> Luke 4:6 (ESV)

This deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed as bluff. Paul does not soften it:

> "In their case the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers, to keep them from seeing the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God."
>
> 2 Corinthians 4:4 (ESV)

Jesus uses a title just as blunt, on his own way to the cross: "now will the ruler of this world be cast out" (John 12:31). Scripture concedes real, if temporary and derivative, dominion over the present fallen order. The offer in the wilderness is not an empty claim to power he does not have. It is a real claim to power that is not, and was never, his to originate. Both things are true at once and need to be held together rather than collapsed into each other: he has genuine reach over a fallen world, and none of that reach makes him the source it came from.

## Someone Else's Word for "I Will"

Set that offer next to a much older sentence, spoken over Nebuchadnezzar's judgment. The king had stood on his own palace roof, looked out over the city, and taken the credit for himself: "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power as a royal residence and for the glory of my majesty?" (Daniel 4:30). The voice that answers him does not merely strip the kingdom away. It corrects the theology behind the boast, stating plainly who actually disposes of thrones:

> "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will and sets over it the lowliest of men."
>
> Daniel 4:17 (ESV)

"Gives it to whom he will." Set beside Luke 4:6's "I give it to whom I will," that is not two authors independently reaching for similar kingship language. It is close enough to be a citation. Daniel states God's own prerogative over every throne on earth, the exact prerogative Nebuchadnezzar forgot he did not hold when he stood on his roof and took credit for Babylon. Satan, in the wilderness, commits the same theft Nebuchadnezzar was judged for, except he commits it in the very sentence built to correct that theft. He places Daniel's language in his own mouth, in the first person, as though the prerogative belonged to him natively rather than the God he is quoting without credit. He does not invent a claim to authority. He steals the sentence that describes the real one and speaks it as his own.

Even the vocabulary of the offer is worth a second look, because it tells you what kind of claim is actually being made. Both Gospels use *basileia*, a kingdom, a territory under a ruler's government, not *ethnos*, the word behind Genesis 10's Table of Nations and "all the *ethnē*" in the Great Commission. This is a claim about political dominion, thrones and administrations, not a claim about every people group across all of history laid out for inspection. Luke's word for "world," *oikoumenē*, is the same word he uses in his own Gospel for the Roman-world census under Caesar Augustus, a census that plainly did not reach the Americas or sub-Saharan Africa or anywhere else outside the empire's own horizon. It points to the known political world of that era rather than a literal survey of every nation across time. Whether the vision included nations not yet founded is a question the text does not answer and should not be pressed to answer, since a plain reading of what was shown could only include what already existed to be shown; what the text does answer, clearly, is that the authority on offer was political and real, not invented and not, in the end, his to give away permanently.

## The Crown He Could Not Deliver

Set the wilderness offer beside its own answer, given after the resurrection:

> "And Jesus came and said to them, 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.'"
>
> Matthew 28:18 (ESV)

Same word, authority. Wider scope, heaven and earth rather than the kingdoms of one era's known world. Opposite path. Satan offered a crown that skipped the cross, glory now, ownership now, worship in exchange for a shortcut around suffering. Christ received the same crown, and more than the same crown, through the cross, from the Father who actually owned it, on the far side of the very death Satan's shortcut was built to avoid. Philippians describes the actual road: he "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Philippians 2:8), and it is only after that, "therefore," that God "highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9). The wilderness offer tried to hand Jesus the destination without the road. The road was never optional, because the road was where the actual purchase happened, and nothing Satan owned was ever going to cover the price.

The thing offered as a shortcut was never a shortcut to anything Satan could deliver. It was already the inheritance, on a different road, through a door Satan held no key to. He gambled a piece of borrowed, temporary territory in exchange for worship. Jesus was already heir to something wider than what was on the table, and refusing the offer cost him nothing he did not already have a truer claim to.

## A Disguise Is Not a Face

The same pattern shows up in smaller places, which is worth naming so the argument does not rest on one scene alone.

> "And no wonder, for even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light."
>
> 2 Corinthians 11:14 (ESV)

A disguise is not a face. It borrows the appearance of something true precisely because the true thing is recognized and trusted, and a copy that did not borrow real material would fool no one. A few verses earlier in the same wilderness scene, Satan quotes real Scripture, Psalm 91, back at Jesus, but quotes it with a piece missing: "He will command his angels concerning you," and "On their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone" (Matthew 4:6). The psalm itself promises exactly that protection, word for word. What Satan leaves out is the psalm's own qualifier a verse earlier, protection promised to the one who walks "in all your ways" (Psalm 91:11), meaning the ways of obedience, not protection promised to anyone willing to jump. Real material, corrupted by omission rather than by invention. The words are accurate. The application is not, and the missing clause is doing all the actual damage.

Paul names the same pattern operating at a wider scale, tying the false signs to a specific kind of victim: "The coming of the lawless one is by the activity of Satan with all power and false signs and wonders, and with all wicked deception for those who are perishing, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved" (2 Thessalonians 2:9-10). The deception does not work on everyone equally. It works on people who have already declined the true thing, which is the same seam the counterfeit bill has: it only fools someone who was not checking closely, or who did not want to find out.

Revelation shows the same pattern in the beast, who "performs great signs, even making fire come down from heaven to earth in front of people," but does so only "by the signs that it is allowed to work" (Revelation 13:13-14). Allowed. Even at its most convincing, spectacular enough to counterfeit fire falling from the sky itself, what gets produced is a likeness of something true, not the thing itself, operating only as far as someone else's permission reaches.

## Checking the Signature

What follows from this is not a catalogue of every counterfeit you might meet. It is a single, transferable question. A convincing claim, a compelling teaching, a spiritual experience that feels unmistakably real is not disqualified by feeling real, since a good counterfeit is supposed to feel real, and the magicians' first three signs felt just as real as Moses' own. The question worth asking is not "does this move me." It is "does this originate anything, or does it only copy and repurpose something that already belonged to someone else."

Paul gives the sharpest version of that question when he tells the Corinthians plainly what actually stands behind worship offered anywhere but to God: "what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Corinthians 10:20). The claim under examination almost never announces itself as borrowed. It never has to. The magicians did not open with "watch us copy this." The beast does not label its fire as permitted rather than original. That is what makes something a counterfeit and not an honest imitation: an honest imitation says what it is. A counterfeit is built entirely around not having to.

That leaves a second, quieter question worth carrying alongside the first, since not every counterfeit shows up as a spectacular sign or a stranger's false teaching. Sometimes it shows up as your own borrowed confidence, a sense of authority or certainty about something that, looked at honestly, was never actually granted to you, only assumed, the way Satan assumed a sentence that belonged to someone else. The test runs the same way in both directions: does this claim originate with the one making it, or was it picked up somewhere and never actually paid for.

A counterfeit bill can fool a register, a hurried clerk, a whole stack of real ones sitting next to it. It cannot fool the mill that made the paper, because the mill knows exactly what it put into the original and exactly what it did not put into the copy. That is the whole account, laid out plainly: three signs that copied cleanly, and then gnats. A kingdom offered with real, derivative authority behind it, and a sentence stolen word for word from the one being who actually holds the authority to speak it. A disguise convincing enough to be called an angel of light, and fire from heaven permitted rather than commanded. Every single instance runs the identical shape. Real material, borrowed without a return address, spent as though it were original, and traceable, every time, back to an owner who never lost the title to it.

Everything Satan has ever held up as his was made somewhere else, by someone else, and it answers, in the end, to that someone. What is borrowed can be called back by the one who owns it. None of it was ever his to keep.
