Half the New Testament's letters survived because somebody walked them across the empire, and Tychicus was Paul's somebody. A native of Asia who joined the team for the great collection journey (Acts 20:4), he became the apostle's most trusted courier: from the Roman imprisonment he carried Ephesians and Colossians together -- and with them Onesimus, the returning slave, and the little letter to Philemon. Three canonical books, one satchel, one dependable man.

Paul's cover note, nearly identical in both letters, doubles as his reference: "Tychicus will tell you all about my activities. He is a beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord. I have sent him to you for this very purpose, that you may know how we are and that he may encourage your hearts" (Colossians 4:7-8; Ephesians 6:21-22). The courier was more than transport: he was the living appendix to the letters, the answerer of questions, the bearer of news and encouragement no parchment could hold.

He stayed useful to the end -- considered for the Crete relief (Titus 3:12), and dispatched to Ephesus in Paul's final letter (2 Timothy 4:12), likely freeing Timothy to come to the dying apostle's side. No sermon, no miracle, no book of his own; just faithful feet, and the entire church's debt every time Ephesians is read.

## In the New Testament

- The collection company (Acts 20:4)
- Bearer of Ephesians and Colossians (Ephesians 6:21-22; Colossians 4:7-9)
- Final errands (Titus 3:12; 2 Timothy 4:12)
