His story is three verses long and runs downhill. In Philemon 24 he is "Demas... my fellow worker," named beside Mark and Luke. In Colossians 4:14, written the same season, the commendation has already thinned: "Luke the beloved physician greets you, as does Demas" -- Demas, no adjective. And in Paul's last letter, from the death cell, the file closes: "Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me and gone to Thessalonica" (2 Timothy 4:10).

Paul's diagnosis is precise and terrible. Not doctrinal collapse, not scandal -- *agapēsas ton nyn aiōna*, "having loved the present age": the same verb John uses for God's love of the world, bent to the opposite object. Demas did not stop believing something; he started loving something else more, at the moment when loving Paul had become dangerous. The letter's counterweight is deliberate: Paul had just written of "all who have loved his appearing" (2 Timothy 4:8) -- two loves, two directions, and Demas chose the age he could see.

Scripture leaves his ending open, as it leaves Simon the sorcerer's and the elder brother's -- open endings being one of the Bible's mercies. Demas remains the New Testament's quiet warning that desertion rarely announces itself with a crisis; it usually just books passage down an ordinary road, for reasons that feel, at the time, entirely sensible.

## In the New Testament

- Fellow worker (Philemon 24; Colossians 4:14)
- The desertion (2 Timothy 4:9-10)
