Peter thought he was being generous: "Lord, how often will my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times?" Jesus' answer -- seventy-seven times, or seventy times seven -- abolished the count altogether, and the parable that follows explains why counting is absurd (Matthew 18:21-22).

A king settling accounts finds a servant owing ten thousand talents -- a deliberately ridiculous figure, something like a laborer's wages for 200,000 years; more than the tax revenue of the whole region. The man's plea ("I will pay you everything") is as absurd as the debt, and the king does not restructure the loan -- "out of pity for him," he *forgives* it. Whereupon the forgiven man walks out, finds a colleague who owes him a hundred denarii -- three months' wages, real money, pocket change by comparison -- seizes him by the throat, ignores the identical plea he had just made, and jails him (Matthew 18:23-30).

The king's question is the parable's point, and it has no answer: "Should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" The unforgiving servant is handed over to the jailers, and Jesus locks the application to every listener: "So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart" (Matthew 18:32-35). Received mercy that does not become extended mercy was never actually received; the arithmetic of grace only runs in one direction -- outward.

## In the New Testament

- The parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35)
- Its echo in the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:12, 14-15)
