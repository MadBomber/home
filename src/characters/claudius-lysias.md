The tribune commanding the Antonia Fortress bought his Roman citizenship "for a large sum" and his Greek name suggests the climb behind it; the Claudius was added with the purchase. Whatever his origins, Acts shows a thoroughly competent officer. When the temple mob set about beating Paul to death, Lysias' quick reaction force saved the apostle's life -- the first of three times he would do so (Acts 21:30-36).

His working theory collapsed twice in an afternoon: the prisoner was not the Egyptian terrorist he assumed ("Do you know Greek?"), and, as the scourging was being rigged, turned out to hold by *birth* the citizenship Lysias had bought dearly -- a revelation that unstrapped Paul instantly and frightened everyone involved (Acts 21:37-39; 22:24-29). Thereafter the tribune did his job well: convened the Sanhedrin to identify the charges, extracted Paul from the ensuing brawl, and, when Paul's nephew exposed the forty-man assassination plot, mounted a midnight extraction to Caesarea with nearly half his garrison -- two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, two hundred spearmen (Acts 23:12-24).

His covering letter to Governor Felix (Acts 23:26-30) is a small comedy of bureaucratic self-presentation -- the timeline gently rearranged so the citizenship discovery precedes the rescue -- but its legal judgment is exactly Luke's theme: "charged with nothing deserving death or imprisonment." The empire's own officers, one after another, kept certifying the gospel's innocence.

## In the New Testament

- The rescue at the temple (Acts 21:30-40)
- The citizenship revelation (Acts 22:22-29)
- The plot and the night ride to Caesarea (Acts 23:12-35)
