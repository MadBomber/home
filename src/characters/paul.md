Saul of Tarsus had the perfect resume for the wrong side: a Roman citizen from a Greek university city, trained in Jerusalem under Gamaliel, "as to righteousness under the law, blameless" -- and so zealous that he made himself the young church's chief predator, "breathing threats and murder" (Philippians 3:4-6; Acts 9:1). The risen Christ dropped him on the Damascus road with a question that fused Christ and church forever: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting *me*?" Three days blind, then baptized, then immediately preaching Jesus in the synagogues he had come to purge (Acts 9:1-22).

The next three decades rewired the Mediterranean. From Antioch he launched three missionary journeys, planting churches across Cyprus, Galatia, Macedonia, Greece, and Asia -- stoned at Lystra, jailed singing at Philippi, mocked at Athens, eighteen months at Corinth, three years at Ephesus -- while fighting the war that mattered most to him: that Gentiles come to Christ by faith alone, without the law's yoke (Acts 13-20; Galatians 2). Arrested in the temple, he spent years defending himself before governors and kings, survived shipwreck, and reached Rome in chains, "proclaiming the kingdom of God... without hindrance" (Acts 21-28).

Thirteen letters carry his name -- from the thunder of Galatians and the cathedral of Romans to the tenderness of Philemon -- written mostly on the road and in cells. His theology never strayed far from his biography: the chief of sinners saved by grace, strength perfected in weakness, a treasure carried in a jar of clay. The farewell stands written from his last prison: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" (2 Timothy 4:7). Tradition says Nero's Rome beheaded him; his letters have outlived the empire by a comfortable margin.

## In the New Testament

- Persecutor (Acts 7:58-8:3; Galatians 1:13)
- The Damascus road (Acts 9; 22; 26)
- The missionary journeys (Acts 13-20)
- Arrest, trials, voyage, Rome (Acts 21-28)
- The thirteen letters, Romans through Philemon
