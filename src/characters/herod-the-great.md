Rome called him King of the Jews -- a title he held for over three decades by ruthless competence. Herod was an Idumean, only half-Jewish by descent, appointed by the Senate and kept in power by results: he rebuilt the temple on a scale that stunned the ancient world, conjured the harbor city of Caesarea from an open coast, and studded the land with fortresses -- Masada, Herodium, Machaerus. The builder's other ledger was written in blood: a wife, three sons, and countless rivals executed on suspicion. Augustus reportedly quipped it was safer to be Herod's pig than his son.

Matthew 2 catches him in his final, most paranoid years. Told by foreign scholars that a king of the Jews had been *born* -- a legitimacy he could never build or buy -- Herod convened the experts, extracted Bethlehem from Micah's prophecy, and played the pious inquirer: "that I too may come and worship him." When the magi slipped away, he ordered every boy in Bethlehem two years and under killed (Matthew 2:16-18). The village was small; the toll perhaps a dozen or two -- unrecorded by historians busy with bigger massacres, and remembered forever because Rachel's weeping made it into Scripture.

He died soon after, rotting and raging, having ordered notables executed on his death day so someone would mourn. The order was ignored. The child he hunted came back from Egypt; the dynasty that tried to kill Christmas survives only as its villain.

## In the New Testament

- The magi and the massacre (Matthew 2:1-18)
- His death and the return from Egypt (Matthew 2:19-22)
- His heirs in the story: Antipas (Luke 13:31-32; 23:6-12), Agrippa I (Acts 12), Agrippa II (Acts 25-26)
