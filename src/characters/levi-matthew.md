Levi son of Alphaeus ran a tax booth in Capernaum -- likely a customs post on the Via Maris, skimming tolls from the trade road under license from Herod Antipas. It made him prosperous and despised: tax collectors were lumped with "sinners" in polite speech and barred as witnesses in court. Jesus walked past the booth, said two words -- "Follow me" -- "and leaving everything, he rose and followed him" (Luke 5:27-28). Fishermen could go back to fishing; a tax farmer who walked off his post was done. Levi burned his boat.

His first act as a disciple was a dinner party: "a great feast in his house," packing his only network -- tax collectors and sinners -- around Jesus (Luke 5:29). The Pharisees' complaint drew one of the Gospels' charter sentences: "Those who are well have no need of a physician... I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance." In the apostle lists he appears as Matthew ("gift of God"), and his own Gospel adds a small self-effacing signature: only Matthew's list remembers to call him "Matthew the tax collector" (Matthew 10:3).

The church's earliest testimony attaches his name to the first Gospel -- a book obsessed with fulfilled Scripture, ordered like a ledger, and generous to outsiders, from Gentile magi in chapter 2 to "make disciples of all nations" in chapter 28. The man whose pen once totaled tolls spent it totaling the evidence that Jesus is Israel's Messiah.

## In the New Testament

- The call at the tax booth (Matthew 9:9; Mark 2:13-14; Luke 5:27-28)
- The feast with tax collectors and sinners (Luke 5:29-32)
- Named among the Twelve (Matthew 10:3; Acts 1:13)
- The Gospel according to Matthew
