## The Big Picture

Matthew stages the opening scene as an echo before Jesus speaks a single
word. He goes up on the mountain. He sits down. His disciples come to Him.
Every piece is deliberate. Exodus 19 and 20 put God on a mountain, giving
law to a people He had just delivered out of slavery, and the crowd that has
followed a healer through Galilee (Matthew 4:23-25) has just followed its own path
out of trouble. The posture matters as much as the location: a seated
teacher in first-century Judea was not resting, he was ruling, the way a
judge sits to hand down a verdict. Moses climbed a mountain to receive a
law. Jesus climbs this one to give one, on His own authority. Everything
that follows in the sermon depends on that single reversal.

The blessings that open the sermon read like a list turned upside down.
Poor, mourning, meek, hungry: these are not qualifications for blessing
anywhere else in the ancient world, yet Jesus names them first. Isaiah had
already promised comfort for mourners and good news for the poor seven
centuries earlier (Isaiah 61:1-3), and Psalm 37 had already promised the land to
the meek rather than the strong (Psalm 37:11). Jesus is not inventing a new
ethic. He is announcing that the kingdom finally belongs to the people the
old order overlooked.

Luke supplies four woes that Matthew never records, and even their shape is
not new: Deuteronomy 27 and 28 pair blessing with curse as the two halves of
one covenant, and the sermon keeps that pairing intact. The week closes
where the mountain scene opened, with authority. Salt and light are not
achievements to reach for; they are what the disciples already are. Verse
17 then supplies the line the rest of the sermon has to answer to: "I have
not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them." Whatever
gets overturned in the weeks ahead, it will not be that.

## Where Luke Puts It

Luke keeps the beatitudes in the same position Matthew does, opening the
sermon (Luke 6:20-23), which is part of why the two accounts read best as one
occasion witnessed twice. But Luke adds something Matthew omits entirely:
four woes matched line for line against the blessings (Luke 6:24-26), rich
against poor, laughing against mourning. Salt and light, by contrast, do
not survive the trip intact. Luke keeps both images but relocates them far
down the road to Jerusalem, salt inside a warning about the cost of
discipleship (Luke 14:34-35) and the lamp inside a saying about spiritual sight
(Luke 11:33). Matthew's version reads as one unbroken movement; Luke's is where
the same pieces resurface exactly when they are needed.

## Shown, Not Just Said

- **The Mountain and the Plain.** Jesus taught with an authority He never
  borrowed, and the apostles carried the same mark long after He was gone:
  "they ... had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13).
- **Blessed Are the Poor in Spirit.** He lived every beatitude before He
  preached it: poor by choice (Matthew 8:20), grieving at a grave (John
  11:35), gentle enough to ride into Jerusalem on a borrowed colt (Matthew 21:5).
- **Blessed Are the Merciful.** The last beatitude promises joy in
  persecution, and the apostles took it literally, "rejoicing that they
  were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (Acts 5:41).
- **Woe to You Who Are Rich.** One man walked away sorrowful rather than
  sell what he owned (Mark 10:21-22); two more died rather than admit what
  they had kept back (Acts 5:1-11).
- **Salt, Light, and the Law.** Jesus called Himself the light of the world
  (John 8:12), then handed the title to the crowd in front of Him (Matthew 5:14),
  and Paul later took it up as his own commission (Acts 13:47).

## Key Themes

- **A New Sinai.** Exodus 19 and 20 put God on a mountain, giving law to a
  people He had just rescued. Matthew stages the sermon the same way on
  purpose, then reverses one thing: this time the man on the mountain gives
  the law Himself.
- **The Kingdom Runs in Reverse.** Poor, mourning, meek, and persecuted
  stand first in line for blessing, exactly where the world would put them
  last.
- **Blessing and Curse Together.** Luke's woes are not padding. Blessing and
  curse have named the two halves of a covenant since Deuteronomy 27 and
  28, and the sermon keeps both.
- **Fulfilled, Not Abolished.** Verse 17 is the promise the rest of the
  sermon has to keep. What gets overturned in the weeks ahead is the
  tradition built on top of the Law, never the Law itself.
