## The Big Picture

Six times in a row, Jesus quotes a command His audience already knew and
then intensifies it. Murder becomes anger. Adultery becomes a look.
Permitted divorce becomes near-total restriction. Regulated oaths become
needless ones. Limited retaliation becomes none. Love of neighbor becomes
love of enemy. Every commandment traces back to the Decalogue itself
(Exodus 20), but the version Jesus is answering has grown case law on top
of the original text, rulings that let a person keep the letter while
missing the point entirely. This week takes that case law apart six times
in a row, and the direction never varies. Nothing gets relaxed.

The clearest evidence for what is actually being overturned sits at the end
of the week. "You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy" (Matthew 5:43)
quotes Leviticus 19:18 for its first half and nothing at all for its
second. No verse anywhere in the Hebrew Scriptures says to hate an enemy.
Jesus is not correcting Moses here. He is correcting a teaching that had
attached itself to Moses, and the fact that half the sentence cannot be
found in the Old Testament is the proof.

Two of the strongest demonstrations in the whole study sit inside this
single week. Isaiah had already described a servant who would open not His
mouth before His accusers (Isaiah 53:7), and Jesus lived that silence out during
His own arrest and trial. Decades later, the same Peter who swung a sword
in the garden wrote instructions for the church built on exactly what he
had watched: "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return" (1 Peter
2:23). The man who failed the teaching became the one who taught it to
everyone else.

## Where Luke Puts It

Luke keeps the payoff of this week and drops most of the case-by-case
argument that gets there. Love your enemies and the call to mercy survive
intact, in the same position in the Sermon on the Plain (Luke 6:27-36), matched
against Matthew's closing command to be perfect (Matthew 5:48) with a different
word: "be merciful, even as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36). But anger,
lust, and oaths never appear in Luke's sermon at all, and divorce surfaces
only much later, in a different setting (Luke 16:18). The other cheek is the one
exception, kept in the sermon in nearly identical wording (Luke 6:29-31). The
pattern from week one continues: Matthew preserves the full legal argument
for a Jewish audience, and Luke keeps what travels.

## Shown, Not Just Said

- **Anger and the Altar.** Jesus prayed for the men killing Him rather than
  answering violence with violence (Luke 23:34), and later restored the
  disciple who had denied Him instead of writing him off (John 21:15-19).
- **The Adulterous Heart.** The Gospels claim He was tempted in every way
  without sin (Hebrews 4:15); no scene proves it, so the honest word here
  is claim, not demonstration.
- **Let Your Yes Be Yes.** Silence before Caiaphas broke only when put
  under oath: "You have said so" (Matthew 26:64). James later turns the
  teaching into a plain command, "let your yes be yes" (James 5:12).
- **The Other Cheek.** Struck and mocked through His own trial, He never
  struck back.
- **Love Your Enemies.** Stephen died praying, "Lord, do not hold this sin
  against them" (Acts 7:60), while Saul stood by holding the coats (Acts
  7:58). The man he asked forgiveness for became the apostle who wrote
  Romans 12.

## Key Themes

- **Six Commands, One Direction.** Anger, lust, divorce, oaths,
  retaliation, enemy love: every antithesis raises the standard. None is
  relaxed.
- **Case Law Versus Law.** What falls this week is the tradition of the
  elders, not the Torah underneath it. Matthew 5:17 from last week is the
  promise this week keeps.
- **A Verse That Proves the Point.** The missing half of Matthew 5:43 turns the
  whole premise from an assertion into something a reader can check.
- **From the Outside In.** Every commandment here starts as a rule about
  visible action and ends as a rule about the heart that produces it.
