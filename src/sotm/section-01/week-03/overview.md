## The Big Picture

Three practices anchor Jewish piety: giving, prayer, and fasting. Jesus does
not question any of them. He questions the audience. Each practice in this
chapter gets performed two ways, once for people watching and once for a
Father who already sees, and the reward for each version is named directly:
applause now, or a Father's attention later. Isaiah had already written the
sharpest version of this argument seven centuries earlier, an entire
chapter contrasting a fast that impresses onlookers with a fast God
actually wants (Isaiah 58:1-12). Isaiah 58 is worth reading before anything else
this week, since it supplies the standard against which every practice here
gets measured.

At the center of the chapter sits the one prayer Jesus taught by name. It
is short enough to memorize in a sitting and complete enough to cover a
lifetime: God's name, God's kingdom, daily need, forgiveness given and
received, deliverance from the test. Daniel had already prayed a longer
version of the same confession and dependence centuries earlier (Daniel 9:4-19),
and the line about forgiveness gets pulled out and repeated on its own
immediately afterward (Matthew 6:14-15), the only line in the whole prayer Jesus
circles back to explain.

Giving in secret is the one practice this week that can never be proven by
an example; a documented case of secret giving stops being secret. That is
not a gap in the material. It is the argument itself, made visible in
Barnabas selling a field and handing over the money (Acts 4:36-37) set
right next to Ananias and Sapphira, who kept some back and lied about it
(Acts 5:1-11).

## Where Luke Puts It

This is the one week where Luke has almost nothing. Giving in secret and
fasting never appear in the Sermon on the Plain at all, and the Lord's
Prayer shows up much later, prompted by a direct request from the disciples
on the road rather than embedded in the sermon (Luke 11:1-4). Three of the five
days this week sit inside Matthew 6:1-18, the most distinctly Jewish block
in the whole sermon, concerned with practices that carried less weight for
the Gentile audience Luke was writing for. The gap argues the thesis rather
than undermining it: what Matthew preserves in full, Luke trims to what
will travel.

## Shown, Not Just Said

- **When You Give.** The practice resists demonstration by definition.
  Barnabas gave without keeping anything back (Acts 4:36-37); Ananias and
  Sapphira gave while keeping everything that mattered (Acts 5:1-11).
- **When You Pray.** Jesus prayed alone before daylight (Mark 1:35) and all
  night before choosing the Twelve (Luke 6:12); the apostles later devoted
  themselves to prayer as a first priority, not a leftover one (Acts 6:4).
- **Our Father in Heaven.** Gethsemane is this prayer prayed under
  pressure. "Not my will, but yours" (Luke 22:42) is "your will be done"
  with the stakes made real.
- **Forgive Us, as We Forgive.** The words from the cross, "Father, forgive
  them" (Luke 23:34), become Paul's plain instruction to the church:
  forgive as the Lord forgave you (Colossians 3:13).
- **When You Fast.** Forty days with no audience but the wilderness
  (Matthew 4:2); the church in Antioch fasted before sending Paul and
  Barnabas out (Acts 13:2-3), and again before appointing elders (Acts 14:23).

## Key Themes

- **An Audience of One.** Giving, prayer, and fasting are measured by who
  is watching, and the only spectator that counts already sees everything.
- **Isaiah Said It First.** Isaiah 58 dismantles performed fasting seven
  centuries before Jesus does, in far more detail.
- **A Prayer Built to Be Prayed, Not Studied.** Five lines cover worship,
  need, forgiveness, and testing; Daniel's longer prayer (Daniel 9:4-19) shows the
  same shape stretched out.
- **The One Line He Explains.** Forgiveness is the only phrase in the
  whole prayer Jesus stops to unpack twice.
