## The Big Picture

Four verses in Leviticus hold almost this entire week before Jesus ever
preaches it: judge your neighbor fairly, do not go about as a slanderer, do
not hate your brother in your heart, love your neighbor as yourself
(Leviticus 19:15-18). Micah had already reduced the whole of religious obligation to
justice, mercy, and humility (Micah 6:8). Jesus is not introducing a new standard
for judgment here. He is holding up the old one and asking why it gets
applied to everyone except the person doing the judging.

The image that follows is almost comic on purpose, a man offering eye
surgery while a piece of lumber sticks out of his own socket. The
exaggeration is the argument: the problem was never noticing a flaw in
someone else. The problem is doing that noticing before dealing with a much
larger one at home. Discernment is never forbidden here, only the sequence
that skips straight past self-examination to someone else's.

Discretion gets its turn next, a warning against handing sacred things to
people determined to trample them, and then the tone shifts entirely toward
confidence: ask, seek, knock, addressed to a Father who gives good gifts
more readily than any human parent could. The section closes with the line
that has outlived the sermon in popular memory, the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12),
stated here in its positive form. Treat others as you want to be treated is
not the same command as the more common ancient version, which only
forbids treating others as you would not want to be treated. The
difference between the two is the difference between avoiding harm and
actively loving, and it is not a small one.

## Where Luke Puts It

Judging and the log in the eye sit in the same position in both accounts
(Luke 6:37-42), argued in almost identical language. Ask, seek, and knock
relocate much further down the road, expanded there into the parable of a
friend pounding on a door at midnight (Luke 11:5-13). Luke has no equivalent at
all for pearls before swine. The Golden Rule survives, but not as a closing
capstone the way Matthew uses it; Luke tucks it into the middle of the
enemies-love passage instead (Luke 6:31), one line among several rather than the
line an entire section builds toward.

## Shown, Not Just Said

- **Judge Not.** Confronted with a woman the law said to stone, He refused
  to condemn her (John 8:7); Peter later had to unlearn the habit of
  calling anyone unclean that God had already accepted (Acts 10:28).
- **The Log in Your Own Eye.** Paul turns the image into direct address:
  "you have no excuse, O man, whoever you are, when you judge another"
  (Romans 2:1).
- **Pearls Before Swine.** Silent in front of Herod, who wanted only a show
  (Luke 23:9); Paul later shook the dust from his feet and moved toward
  audiences that would actually listen (Acts 13:46).
- **Ask, Seek, Knock.** He asked the same request three times in
  Gethsemane before it was answered (Matthew 26:44); the church prayed for
  Peter's release so persistently that when he actually knocked at the
  door, they refused to believe it was him (Acts 12:5-16).
- **The Golden Rule.** He knelt and washed feet that should have been
  washing His (John 13:14-15); the earliest believers held everything in
  common rather than keep track of who owed whom (Acts 2:44-45).

## Key Themes

- **The Whole Week in Four Verses.** Leviticus 19:15-18 already contains
  judgment, slander, hatred, and neighbor-love; the sermon expands what
  Moses had already compressed.
- **Sequence, Not Suppression.** The log and the speck are not an argument
  against noticing faults. They are an argument about which one gets dealt
  with first.
- **Positive, Not Just Negative.** The Golden Rule's active form asks more
  than avoiding harm; it asks for love that moves first.
- **Confidence, Not Hesitation.** Ask, seek, knock assumes a Father eager
  to answer, not one who needs to be worn down.
