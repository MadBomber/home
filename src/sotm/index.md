## Overview

A six-week study of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) read alongside the
Sermon on the Plain (Luke 6:17–49), one teaching preserved by two witnesses.

Both accounts open the same way: Jesus goes up a mountain, sits down, and
teaches. It is the shape of Sinai, and on purpose. This time the one speaking
stands where the LORD stood. At Sinai, Moses went up and *received* the law.
Here, Jesus goes up and *gives* it, on His own authority: "You have heard
that it was said... but I say to you." Moses received. Jesus gives. That
difference is the study's focus.

Each week takes one movement of the sermon with five daily readings and a
discussion guide.

## Matthew and Luke: Witness or Editor?

Luke says up front how he worked, in the only Gospel preface that names a
method at all. "Many have undertaken to compile a narrative of the things
that have been accomplished among us, just as those who from the beginning
were eyewitnesses... it seemed good to me also, having followed all things
closely for some time past, to write an orderly account" (Luke 1:1-4). Luke
was not one of the Twelve. He records their selection just before the
sermon (Luke 6:12-16) without placing himself in the list, and Colossians
4:10-14 sets him apart from "the circumcision," the label Paul uses there
for Jewish believers, which makes Luke very likely a Gentile. He was a
physician (Colossians 4:14) and a longtime companion of Paul (Philemon 24;
2 Timothy 4:11), and the "we" sections of Acts, along with the two years
Paul spent imprisoned at Caesarea (Acts 24:27), put Luke in Judea with time
and access to interview people who had actually been on the mountain.
Luke's sermon is secondhand by his own account, and carefully sourced
secondhand, gathered from people who were there while some of them were
presumably still alive to be asked.

Matthew's position is the opposite. Before he was an apostle he was a tax
collector at Capernaum, called from his booth in the same scene the other
Gospels record under his other name, Levi (Matthew 9:9; Mark 2:14; Luke
5:27). All three lists of the Twelve name him "Matthew the tax collector"
(Matthew 10:3; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15), and Luke's own sequence has Jesus
choosing the Twelve the night before the sermon and coming down to preach
it the next day (Luke 6:12-17). If that order holds, and if the tax
collector and the apostle are the same man, which nearly every ancient and
modern reader agrees on, Matthew was already a disciple and already close
enough to sit among the men who "came to him" when Jesus sat down on the
mountain (Matthew 5:1). Papias, writing in the early second century and
preserved by the church historian Eusebius, credits Matthew directly with
compiling a record of Jesus' sayings, which fits a man who had heard them
firsthand. Matthew's sermon is not an interview. It is a memory.

That difference is the reason two accounts are worth reading side by side
rather than flattened into one. Matthew gives the fuller, more Jewish
version because he was there and wrote for readers who needed the legal
argument spelled out in full. Luke gives the shorter version because he
was reconstructing it from witnesses and wrote for readers who needed the
point more than the case law. Neither is the edited-down copy of the
other. They are two honest records made two different ways, and they agree
on everything that matters.

## The Same Words, Other Voices

Matthew and Luke are not the only New Testament writers who show they knew
this sermon. Its language surfaces again in books written by men who
either heard it themselves or grew up in a church still repeating it.

| Where | Sermon Teaching | The Echo |
|-------|------------------|----------|
| Mark 1:22 | Matthew 7:28-29, authority unlike the scribes | Nearly the same sentence, attached to a synagogue in Capernaum instead of the mountain |
| James 1:22 | Matthew 7:24-27, hearers and doers | "Be doers of the word, and not hearers only" |
| James 2:13 | Matthew 5:7, blessed are the merciful | "Judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy" |
| James 4:11-12 | Matthew 7:1-5, judging | A direct warning against speaking against a brother or judging him |
| James 5:12 | Matthew 5:33-37, oaths | "Let your yes be yes and your no be no," almost word for word |
| 1 Peter 2:12 | Matthew 5:16, let your light shine | Good deeds meant to be seen, so that outsiders glorify God |
| 1 Peter 3:14 | Matthew 5:10, blessed if persecuted | Blessing pronounced on suffering for righteousness' sake |
| Romans 12:14 | Matthew 5:44, love your enemies | "Bless those who persecute you; bless and do not curse" |
| 1 Corinthians 7:10 | Matthew 5:31-32, divorce | Paul credits the command to the Lord rather than to himself |

James, who grew up in the same house as Jesus and did not believe in Him
until after the resurrection (John 7:5; 1 Corinthians 15:7), echoes the
sermon more than any New Testament writer outside the Gospels. Peter, who
was there for at least the Sermon on the Plain (Luke 6:14), quotes the
beatitudes back at his own readers decades later. And Paul, who almost
never quotes Jesus directly, breaks that habit exactly here, on enemies
and on divorce, the two teachings in the sermon hardest to obey. The
pattern argues something without needing to state it outright: this was
not a sermon people admired once and moved on from. It was a sermon the
whole New Testament kept reaching back for.
