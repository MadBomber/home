# Style Guide — Lamplight.Guide Essays

This guide describes the voice, structure, and editorial principles behind the essays published on this site. It exists to help reviewers understand what the writing is doing — and to help the author stay consistent across pieces.

---

## The Purpose of These Essays

These essays are written for readers between roughly eighteen and thirty years old, with no assumed church background, denomination affiliation, or gender. The reader may be skeptical, curious, or somewhere in between. They are probably not regular Bible readers. 

The essays do not manufacture urgency or pressure the reader toward a decision. Where the subject allows, they point to Jesus and His finished work on the cross — because that is always worth pointing to — but the pointing is offered, not pushed.

The essays are also written for the new believer who is learning to read Scripture and needs the vocabulary rebuilt from the ground up.

Both readers are present at the same time. The craft challenge is holding both without losing either.

**The Bible is accessible.** That is the animating conviction behind every essay. When a text reads awkwardly in English, the cause is usually a word that has drifted from its original image or weight. The answer is to go to the original language, recover what the word actually carried, and bring that forward in plain terms. This is not scholarship — it is translation work done in service of the reader who does not have a seminary education and should not need one.

**Answer the unasked question.** When the text assumes knowledge that neither audience is likely to have, supply it briefly and keep moving. "The religious leaders picked up stones" requires a short explanation that stoning was a form of capital punishment — not a digression, just enough to close the gap. The reader should never have to stop and wonder whether they missed something.

---

## Voice and Tone

**Confident and declarative.** The prose does not hedge. It does not say "one might argue" or "it could be said." It says what it means and moves on. When the argument is right, the reader feels it. When the argument is incomplete, the answer is to strengthen it — not to soften it with qualifiers.

**Formal but readable.** Not academic, not casual. The target reader is intelligent and theologically curious but is not reading a commentary. Jargon is avoided unless it is being unpacked. Technical terms (Hebrew words, theological concepts) are introduced and immediately explained.

**Honest about difficulty.** When a text is hard, that hardness is named. The writing does not pretend that everything in Scripture is immediately accessible. It does the work of making it accessible, and it shows that work.

---

## Sentence Structure

The prose mixes short declarative sentences with longer analytical ones. This is intentional and load-bearing — not a stylistic accident.

**Short sentences deliver the blow.** They land like periods, not like ellipses. The reader pauses after each one.

> She was wrong. Not about Romeo. About names.

Those three sentences are doing what a poet does with line breaks. Each period is a beat. Joining them into a single compound sentence — "She was wrong, though not about Romeo — about names" — replaces three blows with one diminished one.

> The word became useless. The reality it names did not.

Two sentences. Two beats. The contrast is structural, not just verbal.

**Fragments are intentional, not errors.** When a sentence is grammatically incomplete, it is because completion would slow it down. "Not about Romeo. About names." is complete in the only way that matters: it delivers the meaning with the right weight.

**The em dash amplifies, not interrupts.** It extends a thought rather than breaking it: "He asked a question — and embedded inside the question was a mischaracterization." The dash leans forward.

**No bullet points in body prose.** Everything flows as paragraphs. If a list is forming, the instinct is to write it as prose and let the rhythm carry the enumeration.

**No bold text for emphasis within flowing paragraphs.** Word choice and sentence structure carry the weight. Bold text signals that the writing does not trust itself to land.

## Word Choice

Precision over smoothness. The right word is chosen for its exact connotation, not for how it sounds in the sentence.

**"Resists translation"** — not "defies translation." *Resists* implies the Hebrew pushes back but can still be approached, that the translator can make progress even if imperfect completion is impossible. *Defies* implies a wall. The distinction matters.

**"Scattered across centuries"** — not "spread across centuries." *Scattered* carries the image of something broadcast at irregular intervals, like seed thrown by hand. *Spread* is neutral and flat. The essay earns its imagery; the right word does not need to announce itself.

**Single verbs over verbal phrases.** Where a phrase can collapse into one precise verb, collapse it. "reads differently" → "differs"; "turn out to name" → "combine to name." Shorter, more direct verbs carry more authority.

### What to Avoid

- **Worn metaphors:** "through the lens of," "window into," "in light of," "shed light on." These feel substantive but are borrowed scenery. Replace with concrete, precise language.
- **Filler modifier phrases:** "for the whole collection," "actually," "in order to," "as we have seen," "building on this." These inflate sentence weight without earning it.
- **Hedging phrases:** "but rather," "one might say," "perhaps," "it could be argued." They soften what should stand on its own.
- **Transitional padding:** Do not announce what the essay is about to say. Say it.

## Scripture

Scripture is always block-quoted. Short quotations — a phrase or clause — may appear inline when the text is quoted exactly and followed immediately by a parenthetical citation: `"be blameless" (Genesis 17:1)`. Full verses belong in a block quote. The verse does work — it is introduced by the argument, not dropped in as decoration.

**Format:**

```
> "Quote text here."
>
> — Book Chapter:Verse (ESV)
```

**Translation:** ESV consistently throughout. When verifying that a quotation matches the ESV exactly, use [esv.org](https://www.esv.org) as the authoritative source. Other sites (BibleGateway, etc.) may carry different text.

**Introduction:** The prose sets up the verse before it appears. The reader knows why they are about to read it. After the verse, the prose unpacks it — it does not assume the verse speaks for itself without engagement.

**Paraphrases and allusions get a reference.** When the prose paraphrases or hints at a passage without quoting it — "the jailer at Philippi was told simply to believe," "In the beginning, the book says" — the passage is identified so the reader can find it. Two forms, chosen by density:

- **Inline parenthetical** for an isolated allusion, placed at a natural pause: `...he was in before morning (Acts 16:30–33).`
- **A footnote** when references would cluster and break the flow — several allusions in one sentence, or back-to-back short sentences that a parenthetical would clutter. Group the related citations into one note. See [Footnotes](#footnotes).

The test is flow: if the inline references would pile up and interrupt the rhythm the prose is built on, move them to a footnote instead.

## Hebrew and Greek

Original-language words appear when the English translation loses something important. They are italicized and defined immediately on first use. They are never used to display scholarship — only to clarify.

> *Peli* — extraordinary, beyond comprehension, too marvelous for asking.

> *Ehyeh asher ehyeh* — the Hebrew resists translation. The literal force is something like "I will be what I will be."

Once defined, the word can be used again without re-definition. It has been given its weight.

## Opening Pattern

Almost every essay begins with a secular provocation, a historical puzzle, or a familiar problem — something the reader already lives with — and then pivots to Scripture as the place where the answer lives.

- *"What's In a Name?"* opens with Juliet's philosophy that names are arbitrary.
- *"Did God Really Say?"* opens with the serpent's question and what it misrepresented.
- *"The Most Useless Word in the English Language"* opens with a direct challenge: define "glory" without using the word.

The opening earns its length by creating a tension that the rest of the essay resolves. It does not exist to show off. It exists to name something the reader already feels.

---

## Structural Pattern

Most essays follow this arc:

1. **Opening provocation** — secular, historical, or linguistic. Names the problem.
2. **Biblical pivot** — Scripture is introduced as the place where the problem is answered.
3. **Historical and textual unpacking** — Hebrew words, context, the progression across centuries. Does the intellectual work.
4. **Application** — second person ("you"), present tense, immediate. Pulls the reader from the textual into the personal.
5. **Return to the opening image** — the essay closes by coming back to what it started with, but the original image has been changed by what the essay did to it.

The closing is not a summary. It is a resolution. The opening image or question reappears transformed.

> Juliet thought names were the least of it... She was half right. What matters is underneath. But the name is how you get there. The name is the door.

## Section Headers

H2 (`##`) level. Punchy and evocative, not academic topic labels.

- "The Thing That Cannot Be Faked" — not "Analysis of Performance vs. Authenticity"
- "The Name He Would Not Give" — not "God's Withholding of His Name in the Old Testament"
- "Before the Child Is Born" — not "Pre-Birth Naming in Scripture"

Headers function as structural signposts, not outlines. A reader should be able to read the headers alone and feel the movement of the essay.

## Footnotes

Used occasionally, for elaborations that are worth making but would interrupt the flow of the argument. The footnote should add a layer of meaning — connect something to something else, press a point further than the prose could — not just explain a citation.

> [^1]: The contrast with John 8:59 is worth pausing on. Those religious leaders bent to the ground and picked up stones to kill Him for claiming the name of God. The stone He gives you is white...

Footnotes are not for pedantic sourcing. They are for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**One exception — grouped Scripture citations.** When the prose leans on several paraphrased or alluded-to passages in a tight cluster (see [Scripture](#scripture)), a footnote collects the references so the inline text stays clean. Use a descriptive slug rather than a bare number, and give each citation a short parenthetical describing what it anchors:

```markdown
He is talking about their descendants, Israel and Edom.[^jacob-esau]

[^jacob-esau]: Genesis 25:23 ("two nations are in your womb"); Malachi 1:2–3 ("Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated," spoken of the peoples Israel and Edom).
```

**Placement.** Kramdown renders every footnote as a single list at the very bottom of the page — *below* the References section — no matter where the `[^slug]:` definitions sit in the source. So an essay that uses footnotes must end with a `## Footnotes` section header, added as the last line of the file, to give that rendered list a heading. Put the definitions themselves under that header (source order then matches render order: essay → `## References` → `## Footnotes` → definitions).

The References section still lists every passage the essay touches; the footnotes are the in-context pointer, References is the full ledger.

## Front Matter

```yaml
---
layout: blog_post
title: "Title Here"
description: One or two sentences. Present tense. Reads as a statement, not a topic announcement.
date: YYYY-MM-DD
author: Dewayne VanHoozer
collection: collection-slug   # optional, for multi-part series
order: N                       # optional, position in collection
---
```

The description field is the reader's first encounter with the essay. It should state the argument, not describe it: "Juliet called names arbitrary. Scripture calls them declarations." — not "This essay examines the significance of names in Scripture."

---

## References

Every essay ends with a references section:

```markdown
## References

- *The Holy Bible, English Standard Version* (ESV). Crossway, 2001.
- [Other sources as applicable]
```

References is the last *prose* section — except when the essay uses footnotes, in which case a `## Footnotes` header follows it as the final line of the file (see [Footnotes](#footnotes)).

---

## A Note for Reviewers

This writing uses short sentences, rhetorical fragments, and deliberate white space as structural tools. When a sentence seems incomplete, the question to ask is not *is this grammatically correct?* but *is this doing what it intends to do?*

The prose is not trying to be smooth. It is trying to be true, clear, and weighted in the right places. Smoothing it often costs it exactly the thing it was built for.

The best review question is: *does the argument land?* If a sentence is unclear, if a verse is not connected to the argument around it, if the opening hook does not pay off in the closing — those are the things worth flagging.
